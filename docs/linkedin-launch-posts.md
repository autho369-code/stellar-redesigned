## 1 · Section 22.1 disclosures

*Links to `/blog/illinois-section-22-1-disclosure-board-guide`*

> Most Illinois condo boards treat the 22.1 disclosure as paperwork. It isn't.
>
> When a unit sells, the seller has to hand the buyer a package covering the association's finances, insurance, pending litigation, planned capital expenditures, and any unpaid balances. The request lands on the management desk, and there's a statutory clock on the response.
>
> Get it wrong — miss the window, understate a pending assessment, omit litigation — and the exposure doesn't sit with the seller. It sits with the association.
>
> The part boards underestimate: a 22.1 response is a written statement about the association's financial condition, made to someone about to spend several hundred thousand dollars on the strength of it. That deserves the same care as the audit.
>
> I wrote up what belongs in the packet, who assembles it, and how to build a response workflow that doesn't depend on one person remembering.
>
> https://www.stellarpropertygroup.com/blog/illinois-section-22-1-disclosure-board-guide

## 2 · Reserve funds

*Links to `/blog/condo-reserve-funds-explained`*

> Every special assessment I've seen in nineteen years was a reserve decision made years earlier, catching up.
>
> The pattern is consistent. A board keeps assessments flat because owners don't want an increase. The reserve study sits in a drawer. The roof reaches the end of its life on schedule — roofs are not surprising — and suddenly it's $400,000 that nobody has.
>
> Illinois requires condo associations to fund reserves that are reasonable for repairing and replacing common elements, judged against the age and condition of the building. The statute deliberately doesn't give a number. That's what the reserve study is for: component inventory, remaining useful life, replacement cost, converted into a funding plan.
>
> An underfunded reserve isn't saving owners money. It's deferring a bill and adding interest, and it shows up in resale value long before the repair does.
>
> https://www.stellarpropertygroup.com/blog/condo-reserve-funds-explained

## 3 · Special assessments

*Links to `/blog/condo-special-assessments-illinois`*

> "Why am I suddenly being asked for $18,000?"
>
> It's the hardest question an owner asks, and the honest answer is usually uncomfortable: because the work was coming for a decade and the association collected as though it wasn't.
>
> Special assessments feel arbitrary from the owner's side. They rarely are. They're the visible moment in a slow sequence — a deferred repair, a reserve study nobody funded, a system that aged exactly as predicted.
>
> For boards, the useful takeaway isn't about the current assessment. It's that the next one is already forming, and it's still cheap to prevent.
>
> This guide covers how Illinois special assessments work, what owner rights attach to them, and the funding decisions that determine whether your association needs one at all.
>
> https://www.stellarpropertygroup.com/blog/condo-special-assessments-illinois

## 4 · Owner vs. association repairs

*Links to `/blog/condo-repairs-owner-vs-association-illinois`*

> The single most common dispute in condo management: a pipe leaks, and nobody agrees who pays.
>
> The answer is in the declaration, not in what a neighbor was told or what happened last time. Broadly: the association maintains common elements — roof, facade, structure, common risers, boilers, elevators. Owners maintain what sits inside their unit boundaries. Limited common elements like balconies and assigned parking often follow their own rules.
>
> But "broadly" is doing real work in that sentence. Declarations vary building to building, and the expensive arguments happen exactly where they differ.
>
> The practical advice for boards: figure out where your declaration draws the line *before* a leak, write it down in plain language, and give it to owners. Nobody reads a declaration at 11pm with water coming through the ceiling.
>
> https://www.stellarpropertygroup.com/blog/condo-repairs-owner-vs-association-illinois

## 5 · Comparing management proposals

*Links to `/blog/score-condo-hoa-management-proposals`*

> If your board is collecting management proposals, they will all sound the same. That's a feature of how they're written, not a coincidence.
>
> Two things cut through it.
>
> First, normalize the money. Convert every proposal to an annual per-unit cost including everything billed separately — capital project oversight, transition fees, statutory disclosure charges, after-hours calls. A low base fee with unbundled extras frequently costs more than a higher all-in quote.
>
> Second, ask all of them the same four operational questions: Who specifically is our manager? How many associations do they carry? What arrives each month, and on what date? Who answers the phone at 2am?
>
> Proposals rarely differ on what they promise. They differ enormously on those four answers.
>
> Full scoring framework, including a comparison sheet your board can use directly:
>
> https://www.stellarpropertygroup.com/blog/score-condo-hoa-management-proposals

## 6 · Which Illinois law applies

*Links to `/blog/which-illinois-law-governs-condo-hoa-townhome`*

> A surprising number of Illinois boards are following the wrong statute.
>
> Condominiums fall under the Illinois Condominium Property Act. Homeowner and townhome associations that aren't condominiums generally fall under the Common Interest Community Association Act — and smaller communities are sometimes exempt from parts of it. Both sit alongside the General Not For Profit Corporation Act and your own recorded declaration.
>
> This isn't academic. Notice periods, records-inspection rights, voting thresholds, and board meeting requirements all differ between them. A townhome board running its annual meeting on condo rules is creating a challengeable election, and usually has no idea.
>
> If your board has never explicitly confirmed which act governs it, that's worth twenty minutes at the next meeting.
>
> https://www.stellarpropertygroup.com/blog/which-illinois-law-governs-condo-hoa-townhome
